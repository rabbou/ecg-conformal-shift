# Predicting an ECG model's positive predictive value from a clinic's prevalence: a retrospective measurement across hospitals and care settings

Ruben Abbou · October 2026

## Abstract

**Background.** A clinic that adopts an ECG model receives a sensitivity and a specificity measured on another hospital's patients. To learn its positive predictive value (PPV), the share of flagged patients who are truly ill, the usual advice is to apply Bayes' theorem at the clinic's own prevalence, the share of its patients who are ill. That recomputation is exact only if sensitivity and specificity carry over unchanged.

**Methods.** A threshold set to flag 90% of the ill in a source population was applied unchanged to a target population in 174 transfers: four structural heart disease models carried from Columbia inpatients to its emergency patients and outpatients (EchoNext), an infarction network carried from a German database to two Chinese hospitals, and networks for five rhythm and conduction diagnoses rotated across five corpora from three countries. In each transfer, the PPV recomputed from the source's sensitivity and specificity at the target's true prevalence was compared with the PPV observed among flagged target patients; 72 controls read each model on held-out patients of its own source. At Columbia, the threshold was also set again on outpatients with known diagnoses, and three corrections were judged on net benefit.

**Results.** The recomputed PPV missed the observed one by a median of 5.3 (4.2 to 7.8) percentage points, against 0.3 (0.2 to 1.0) on controls, and fell outside the observed PPV's 95% confidence interval in 75% (67% to 82%) of transfers, too high in some and too low in others. At Columbia, 25.6% (23.1% to 28.3%) of outpatients were ill and 46.0% (41.3% to 50.7%) of those the trained network flagged were ill; the recomputation gave 34.2%. The network separated ill from healthy outpatients almost as well as inpatients (AUROC 0.815 and 0.805), but ill and healthy outpatients both scored lower, so the inpatient threshold caught 71.6% (65.9% to 76.6%) of ill outpatients and flagged 29 of 100 healthy ones, against 90.4% and 58 among inpatients. The share of outpatients flagged, 39.8% (36.9% to 42.8%), was lower than any prevalence could produce with the inpatients' sensitivity and specificity. Set again on 858 separate outpatients, the threshold caught 86.3% (81.7% to 89.9%) of ill outpatients and flagged 52 of 100 healthy. At Chongqing the recomputation erred the other way: 45% predicted, 26% (25% to 27%) observed.

**Conclusion.** A model's PPV at a new clinic is not reliably predicted from the clinic's prevalence: the model's ordering of patients carries over, but the point where a fixed threshold lands does not. A clinic learns its PPV by counting it on its own patients with known diagnoses. The share of patients it flags, which needs no diagnosis, shows early that the threshold has moved.

## 1. Introduction

A hospital evaluating an ECG model receives an AUROC, a sensitivity and a specificity measured on someone else's patients. The AUROC is the chance that a randomly chosen ill patient scores higher than a randomly chosen healthy one. Sensitivity is the share of ill patients the model flags at its threshold, the cut-off on its score; specificity is the share of healthy patients it leaves unflagged. None of these says how many of a clinic's alerts will be real. That share is the positive predictive value (PPV), and its counterpart, the negative predictive value (NPV), is the share of unflagged patients who are healthy. Both depend on the prevalence, the share of the clinic's patients who have the disease. The usual advice, which the author has given too, is to recompute the PPV at the local prevalence with Bayes' theorem: PPV = sens × prev / (sens × prev + (1 − spec) × (1 − prev)).

The recomputation is exact when only the prevalence differs between two populations and the ill and healthy patients are otherwise alike. Machine learning calls this label shift: the share of each diagnosis moves, while the ECGs within each diagnosis look the same [5]. When the ECGs themselves differ, which is called covariate shift, or when the patients counted as ill or healthy differ, sensitivity and specificity move and the recomputation goes wrong. Both are kinds of dataset shift, a difference between the patients a model was validated on and those it meets in use. Clinical epidemiology names the same problem the spectrum effect: a test's sensitivity and specificity depend on which ill and which healthy patients it is measured on [2,3]. Across 23 meta-analyses, the sensitivity or specificity of one test differed by up to 40 points between low- and high-prevalence studies, and specificity tended to fall as prevalence rose [4].

Sometimes the recomputation works. A hyperkalemia model validated at Mayo Clinic reached 80% sensitivity and 80% specificity in the emergency department, where 1% of patients had a potassium above 6.0 mEq/L and the PPV was 3%, and 82% and 82% in intensive care, at 3% prevalence and a PPV of 14% [1]. With sensitivity and specificity nearly unchanged between the two units, Bayes' theorem carried from one to the other lands close to what was observed. For structural heart disease the evidence leans the other way. In the EchoNext study, at a fixed 70% sensitivity, specificity at the external hospitals was 10 points lower than at Columbia [6]. On their multicentre test set from eight NewYork-Presbyterian hospitals, EchoNext's authors report for the full model an AUROC of 0.843 among outpatients and 0.841 among inpatients and describe consistent performance across care settings [6]; the public EchoNext set used here comes with no AUROC by care setting. Their table by care setting gives the AUROC and the area under the precision-recall curve, and the F1 score and diagnostic odds ratio at a fixed cut-off of 0.5, but no sensitivity or specificity; the F1 score, which falls with prevalence, was 0.557 among outpatients against 0.728 among inpatients. At another hospital, the published mini-model separated almost as well, with an AUROC of 0.790 against 0.820 on the EchoNext test patients, and by care setting 0.796 among outpatients against 0.773 among inpatients and intensive care patients, with no sensitivity reported at a fixed cut-off [7]. In a community cohort of people aged 65 to 85 the AUROC fell to 0.71, from 0.83 in hospital, which the authors attribute to milder disease [8]. An AUROC that holds says nothing about whether a threshold holds.

Thresholds that do not travel are not new. The Mayo Clinic model for an ejection fraction of 35% or less still separated ill from healthy well in a Russian population sample, with an AUROC of 0.82, yet the cut-off chosen in its original study caught only 26.9% of those with the condition; its authors concluded that each population may need its own cut-off [9]. The opposite has been seen too: across four American sites and 13,960 patients, a model for an ejection fraction of 40% or less, at a cut-off fixed in advance, was 84.5% sensitive and 83.6% specific, and its performance did not differ significantly between sites [10]. PRESENT-SHD, a structural heart disease model whose threshold was set for 90% sensitivity at one hospital, caught 92.5% to 96.0% of the ill at four others and 87.5% in a Brazilian population cohort [11]. In pathology and CT, groups have reset cut-offs on a handful to 25 local cases [12,13]. In screening mammography, a commercial model's cut-off was reset on 16,204 local screens, 200 of them with cancer, and each version of the mammography software needed its own [14]. Searches of PubMed and Europe PMC on 5 October 2026 (supplement S1.13) found no study that measured, for structural heart disease, what happens to a fixed threshold when one hospital's model moves from its inpatients to its outpatients.

This study asks whether a clinic can predict a model's PPV from its own prevalence. It counts how far the recomputed PPV lands from the observed one in 174 transfers of a threshold between hospitals, care settings and corpora, uses Columbia's move from inpatients to outpatients to show why it misses, and measures what a site can do about it.

## 2. Methods

A transfer is one model, one diagnosis and one threshold, set on a source population and applied unchanged to a target. Every transfer was read on public, de-identified ECGs whose diagnoses are known, so the observed PPV could be counted.

### 2.1 Patients and ECGs at Columbia

EchoNext v1.1.1 holds 100,000 ECGs from Columbia University Irving Medical Center and the Allen Hospital, two NewYork-Presbyterian sites called Columbia below, each paired with a transthoracic echocardiogram; the files do not record which site an ECG came from [6]. The condition studied is moderate or worse structural heart disease on echocardiography, which EchoNext defines as any of eleven findings: a left ventricular ejection fraction of 45% or less; a left ventricular wall thickness of 1.3 cm or more; moderate or worse aortic stenosis, aortic regurgitation, mitral regurgitation, tricuspid regurgitation or pulmonary regurgitation; moderate or worse right ventricular systolic dysfunction; a moderate or large pericardial effusion; a pulmonary artery systolic pressure of 45 mmHg or more; and a tricuspid regurgitation peak velocity of 3.2 m/s or more. An ECG is called ill when it was recorded within a year before an echocardiogram showing any of the eleven, and healthy when it was recorded at any time before the patient's most recent echocardiogram showing none: EchoNext puts no time limit on the healthy [6]. In the test split, 27.9% of the healthy outpatients and 10.9% of the healthy inpatients carry no echocardiographic measurement, which EchoNext records only within the year, so their ECG was taken earlier (supplement S1.2).

EchoNext assigns its ECGs to a training, a validation and a test group, and leaves 17,457 in none, which this study does not use; no patient is in two of the three groups. The models learned from the 72,475 ECGs of the training group, taken from 26,218 patients, several ECGs each. In the validation and test groups each patient contributes one ECG, their latest. The thresholds were set on the 1,903 inpatient ECGs of the validation group, 53.2% of them from ill patients; these are called the calibration inpatients below. Everything was then measured on the test group: 1,059 outpatient ECGs (25.6% ill), and beside them 2,203 other inpatient ECGs (52.5% ill) and 1,971 emergency ECGs (40.0% ill). EchoNext records a fourth setting, procedural, whose 209 test ECGs are not analysed; supplement S1.2 counts every ECG and says which were used. The ECGs were recorded between 2008 and 2022, from patients aged 18 or over. The outpatients' ECGs are older than the inpatients' (median year 2015 against 2017), and within each band of years the outpatients' sensitivity stayed well below the inpatients' (66.2% to 82.3% against 88.9% to 92.2% over three bands and three models; supplement S1.2).

### 2.2 Other hospitals and corpora

PTB-XL is a German research database of ECGs recorded between 1989 and 1996 [15]. An infarction network trained on it was carried to Shandong Provincial Hospital [16] and to the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University [17]. At PTB-XL an infarction is an infarct pattern read on the tracing, most often without a stated stage; at Shandong it is read on the tracing too, and most are coded as old; at Chongqing it is an acute infarction named in the discharge diagnosis of patients who all had coronary angiography.

Five corpora were rotated through the source role on five rhythm and conduction diagnoses: sinus rhythm, atrial fibrillation, left bundle-branch block, right bundle-branch block and first-degree atrioventricular block. The corpora are PTB-XL, Shandong, and three read from the PhysioNet/Computing in Cardiology Challenge 2021 collection [18]: Chapman-Shaoxing with Ningbo (China), Georgia (United States) and CPSC 2018 with its extension (China). Each corpus was split by patient, after merging records that file the same tracing twice, and its training and calibration parts were capped to the size of the smallest corpus (supplement S2.4).

### 2.3 Models

At Columbia four models score every ECG. The first is a deep neural network (a residual network) trained on EchoNext for this study. The second is the published EchoNext mini-model, run on its authors' weights with nothing changed; on the EchoNext test patients it reaches an AUROC of 0.820, the figure its authors report [6] and Otabor and colleagues quote [7]. The third is ECGFounder, a network pre-trained on more than ten million ECGs from another hospital [19]. Its network was kept as published; it turns each ECG into a list of numbers, and a simple statistical formula (a logistic regression) fitted on EchoNext turns that list into a score for each finding. The fourth is the first network with its weights left at random, read through the same kind of formula: a floor that any useful model must beat. The Columbia results compare the first three. For infarction, a residual network was trained on PTB-XL; for the rotation, one residual network was trained on each corpus. The [supplement](SUPPLEMENT.md) gives the architectures, how the published weights were read, and the statistics behind every method below.

### 2.4 The threshold

For each model and diagnosis, the threshold was placed among the ill patients of the source so that 90% of them scored at or above it. A patient above it is flagged, and a patient below it is cleared. With few ill patients the threshold is placed slightly lower than the exact 90% point, so that it errs toward catching more (supplement S1.1).

At Columbia the source is the calibration inpatients: the case of a model validated in hospital and then offered to clinics. The same rule was also set on two other groups of validation patients: all 4,626 of them whatever the setting, as a vendor validating on everyone with an echocardiogram might, and the 858 validation outpatients, 240 of them ill, who are not the outpatients the results are read on. For infarction, the threshold was set on half of PTB-XL's held-out patients and applied unchanged to the other half, to Shandong and to Chongqing; this was repeated with 200 random halves, and each figure is the average. In the rotation, each corpus set the threshold on its calibration part, and the threshold was applied to the test part of each of the other four, over 200 draws of the calibration part.

### 2.5 The recomputed and the observed PPV

In each transfer, the source's sensitivity and specificity at the threshold, put into Bayes' theorem with the target's true prevalence, give the recomputed PPV. The observed PPV is the share of flagged target patients who are ill, with a Wilson 95% confidence interval. Giving the recomputation the true prevalence is generous, since a clinic would have to estimate it. The gap is the recomputed PPV minus the observed one, in percentage points, with an interval from 2,000 redraws of both the source and the target counts. A transfer is summarised when its target holds at least 10 ill patients and its threshold flags at least 20, so that the observed PPV has an interval narrower than the gaps in question; 24 transfers fell short.

At Columbia the transfers are four models, eleven findings and their composite, from the calibration inpatients to the emergency patients and to the outpatients. Controls read the same models on held-out patients of their own source: Columbia's calibration inpatients to the inpatients of the test split, and each rotation corpus to its own test part. Transfers that share a model and a pair of populations are not independent, so each share across transfers carries a 95% interval that resamples whole groups of them, 30 groups for the transfers and 9 for the controls (supplement S3.1).

If only the prevalence had changed between two populations, a model recalibrated on the source would give its healthy patients the same likelihood ratio on average in both, close to 1. The likelihood ratio here is the odds the model gives a patient divided by the source's prevalence odds. Its median over diagnoses and models, among the healthy of each target, was computed as a check of label shift.

Three predictions of the PPV that need no local diagnosis were also scored against the observed PPV: the recomputation at a prevalence estimated from the site's unlabelled scores, the model's own mean probability among the flagged, and that mean probability corrected to the estimated prevalence.

### 2.6 What was counted among Columbia patients

For 100 ill patients: caught, meaning flagged by the threshold, and missed. For 100 healthy patients: cleared and flagged. PPV and NPV are given at the outpatients' own prevalence. A range in brackets after a percentage is its 95% confidence interval, which resamples the patients read for the threshold as set; where the calibration patients are resampled as well, the text says so. A range across repetitions is named as such.

To see how well each model separates ill from healthy in each setting, the AUROC was computed, and a curve drawn of ill caught against healthy flagged at every possible threshold. The three models were compared on the same outpatients.

A different case mix among outpatients could explain part of the change. To measure how much, a formula fitted on the ill patients of both settings estimated each patient's chance of being caught from their findings, ejection fraction, age, sex and setting. Applied to the ill inpatients as if they were outpatients, it gives the outpatients' sensitivity had they carried the inpatients' case mix (supplement S1.6). Differences between women and men and between age groups were tested with a correction for running six tests at once (supplement S1.8); they are exploratory.

### 2.7 What a site can do

Three repairs were applied to the same scores in every transfer with enough patients. The first uses no diagnosis: it estimates the site's prevalence from its unlabelled probabilities by the maximum-likelihood method of Saerens and colleagues [5], after recalibrating the model on the source as Alexandari and colleagues advise [20], and moves every probability to that prevalence. The second is logistic recalibration on local labels: an intercept and a slope fitted on 100 local ECGs with known diagnoses, or the intercept alone when the draw holds fewer than 10 of the rarer class [21]. The third adds a second, higher threshold set on the healthy source patients, so that patients between the two thresholds go to a human reader (section 3.8). Each repair was read on one half of the target cut by patient, with the other half as the pool the labelled repair drew from, over 200 draws.

The repairs were judged on net benefit, the measure of decision curve analysis [22]. A clinician who would send a patient to echocardiography above a 10% probability of disease accepts nine normal studies for one abnormal one. Net benefit counts the true positives per patient minus the false positives weighted by those odds, and compares the model with sending everyone and sending no one. It was computed at decision thresholds of 5%, 10% and 20%.

At Columbia, the threshold was also set again on outpatients with known diagnoses: threshold recalibration on local labels, in which the model's weights stay as they are and only the cut-off moves. The outpatients were split at random into two halves; 100 patients with known diagnoses were picked from the first half, the threshold was set again on them, and the result was read on the second half. This was repeated 2,000 times with a new split each time, and each repetition noted how many ill patients its 100 held. Those 100 come from the outpatients the result is read on. Local validation, the measurement of a model on a site's own patients, needs patients the threshold never saw, so the threshold was also set on all 858 validation outpatients, and on 100 drawn from them 2,000 times, and each was read on every test outpatient.

## 3. Results

The PPV recomputed by Bayes' theorem missed the observed one in most transfers, in both directions. At Columbia the model's ordering of patients held while the outpatients' scores moved down, so the threshold landed at another point and sensitivity and specificity moved.

### 3.1 The recomputed PPV missed in both directions

Across 174 transfers, the recomputed PPV missed the observed one by a median of 5.3 (4.2 to 7.8) percentage points. It fell within two points in 25% (18% to 33%) of transfers and outside the observed PPV's 95% confidence interval in 75% (67% to 82%). On the 72 controls it missed by 0.3 (0.2 to 1.0) points and fell outside the interval in 1% (0% to 5%). The gap also matters relative to the PPV itself: the recomputed PPV was off by a quarter or more of the observed one in 57% (46% to 69%) of transfers, against 1% (0% to 4%) of controls.

### 3.2 Columbia: the ordering of patients holds, the threshold lands elsewhere

At Columbia, 25.6% (23.1% to 28.3%) of the test outpatients had moderate or worse structural heart disease. Of the outpatients the trained network flagged with its inpatient threshold, 46.0% (41.3% to 50.7%) were ill. Among the calibration inpatients the threshold caught 90.1% of the ill and left 40.4% of the healthy unflagged; Bayes' theorem with those two figures at the outpatients' prevalence gives 34.2%, 11.7 points too low (8.3 to 15.3). The recomputation implied 1.9 false alerts per ill patient found where there were 1.2. For an ejection fraction of 45% or less it predicted that 14.4% of flagged outpatients would have one, and 31.8% (25.6% to 38.7%) did: 5.9 false alerts per patient found predicted, 2.1 observed.

Sensitivity and specificity both moved. Set to catch 90% of the ill calibration inpatients, the trained network's threshold caught 90.4% of the ill among the other inpatients (88.6% to 92.0%) and 71.6% of ill outpatients (65.9% to 76.6%). Redrawing the calibration inpatients as well, so that the threshold moves too, widens the outpatient interval to 65.2% to 78.0%. Emergency patients sat between, at 86.2%. It also flagged fewer healthy patients: 29 of 100 healthy outpatients, against 58 of 100 healthy among the other inpatients; its specificity was 71.1% (67.8% to 74.1%) among outpatients and 42.2% (39.3% to 45.2%) among inpatients. The published mini-model caught 72.7% and ECGFounder 71.6% of ill outpatients.

In a clinic seeing 1,000 outpatients at Columbia's outpatient prevalence, the inpatient threshold sends 398 for an echocardiogram, finds 183 of the 256 ill and misses 73. Of the outpatients it clears, 87.9% (85.2% to 90.2%) are healthy.

The model did not get worse at telling ill from healthy outpatients. The AUROC of the trained network was 0.815 among inpatients (0.796 to 0.832), 0.805 among outpatients (0.774 to 0.837) and 0.837 among emergency patients (0.820 to 0.854). For the mini-model it moved from 0.797 to 0.795, and for ECGFounder from 0.804 to 0.791. Figure 2 draws, for every possible threshold, how many ill it catches against how many healthy it flags. The inpatient and outpatient curves lie within a few points of each other: catching 90 ill in 100 flags 57 and 59 healthy in 100, and catching 72 flags 25 and 31. The same threshold lands at a different point on that curve: among inpatients it caught 90 ill in 100 and flagged 58 healthy in 100; among outpatients it caught 72 and flagged 29. Ill and healthy outpatients both scored lower than their inpatient counterparts. The AUROC compares ill with healthy patients within one setting, and because both groups moved together it barely changed; the PPV recomputed from the inpatients' figures assumed a specificity the outpatients did not have.

On the same ill outpatients, the trained network caught exactly as many as ECGFounder and 1.1 points fewer than the mini-model. Each pair's 95% interval lies within 5.5 points of zero (supplement, Table S2); the three intervals hold at 95% each, not jointly. The untrained floor caught more ill outpatients, 78.6%, only because it flagged far more healthy ones: 43 in 100, against 29 for the trained network. All three models learned from the same EchoNext patients, so they are not three independent tests of the cause. A score built on age and sex alone, fitted on the training group and thresholded the same way, caught 90.4% of ill inpatients and 89.7% of ill outpatients: its sensitivity did not fall, so the fall does not come from the outpatients' age and sex; it comes with what their ECGs show. That score separated poorly (AUROC 0.678 among outpatients) and flagged 73 of 100 healthy outpatients, where the ECG models, with every diagnosis known, flag 58 to 65 at 90 caught.

Where the threshold is set changes how far it lands. Set on all 4,626 validation patients, whatever their setting, it caught 77.1% of ill outpatients (71.8% to 81.7%) and 94.0% of ill inpatients; for the mini-model and ECGFounder, 76.4% and 77.9% of ill outpatients. The fall is smaller from that threshold, and it remains.

### 3.3 Why the outpatients scored lower

The loss was not spread evenly over the eleven findings. Among ill patients with an ejection fraction of 45% or less, the threshold caught 91.4% of outpatients (64 of 70) against 94.2% of inpatients, and among those with aortic stenosis 94.1% against 97.2%. For a wall thickness of 1.3 cm or more it caught 69.2% of outpatients (101 of 146) against 91.9%; for mitral regurgitation 70.0% against 95.4%, for tricuspid regurgitation 69.2% against 94.0%, and for a pulmonary pressure of 45 mmHg or more 71.4% against 91.0%. Of the 77 ill outpatients it missed, 59 had a single finding, a thick wall alone for 35 of them; 12 had an ejection fraction of 35% or less or a severe grade. Leaving out the ill whose only finding was a thick wall, it caught 77.4% of ill outpatients against 91.6% of ill inpatients. A threshold set separately for each finding gives a similar picture (supplement, Table S3).

The ill outpatients were ill differently from the ill inpatients. Among the ill, the median number of the eleven findings was 2 for the calibration inpatients and 1 for outpatients, and the median ejection fraction 50.0% against 57.5%; the outpatients had less right ventricular and tricuspid disease, but a thicker septum and more aortic stenosis (supplement S1.7). The case-mix model compares the outpatients with the test inpatients. The trained network's sensitivity fell by 18.8 points from inpatients to outpatients. Had the ill outpatients carried the inpatients' findings, ejection fraction, age and sex, it would have been 76.8% instead of 71.6%: 5.2 of the 18.8 points, 27.5% of the fall (16% to 38%); read as the difference between two of the model's own predictions, the share is 25.2% (13% to 36%). For the other two models the share was 27.5% and 25.3%. Case mix explains about a quarter of the fall, and most of it happens among patients with the same findings, ejection fraction, age and sex.

Among the ill outpatients, the trained network caught 61.1% of women (77 of 126) and 80.7% of men (117 of 145), and 53.3% of patients aged 18 to 49 (16 of 30) against 87.3% of those aged 80 and over. Both differences were larger than chance would explain after correcting for six tests (p = 0.002 for sex and 0.002 for age), and the other two models showed the same two differences (corrected p no higher than 0.041). The same threshold left more healthy women and young outpatients unflagged: its specificity was 78.9% among healthy women against 59.4% among healthy men, and 80.3% at 18 to 49 against 46.8% at 80 and over, while the AUROC of women (0.773, 0.719 to 0.826) and of men (0.827, 0.784 to 0.871) overlapped. Women and younger outpatients scored lower, ill and healthy alike, which is the main result's mechanism inside the outpatients. In the 5 race and ethnicity groups EchoNext records with 20 or more ill outpatients, the threshold caught 70.6% to 72.7% of ill outpatients against 89.1% to 91.7% of ill inpatients. These are exploratory readings in one hospital system.

### 3.4 Across hospitals, the model's separation changes too

The infarction threshold, set to catch 90% of infarctions at PTB-XL, caught 89.6% there (84.0% to 95.1%), 83.7% at Chongqing (81.2% to 86.1%) and 97.3% at Shandong (95.4% to 99.3%). Each range spans 95% of what one threshold set this way would catch: at PTB-XL it is the spread of the 200 draws, each read on about 276 infarctions; at Chongqing and Shandong, read whole in every draw, it adds the sampling of the site's own infarctions. Its specificity was 81.0% at PTB-XL, 57.9% at Chongqing and 82.9% at Shandong.

### 3.5 The share flagged warns without a diagnosis

Without diagnoses, a clinic sees how many patients the model flags. From inpatients to outpatients, the share flagged by the trained network fell from 74.9% (73.0% to 76.7%) to 39.8% (36.9% to 42.8%). Fewer ill patients alone cannot produce that fall. Had the inpatients' sensitivity and specificity held, the share flagged would have been 66.1% at the outpatients' prevalence, and no prevalence could take it below 57.8%, the share of healthy inpatients flagged. The mini-model flagged 40.1% of outpatients against a floor of 61.2%, and ECGFounder 39.7% against 57.2%. A clinic that compares its share flagged with the validated one therefore sees that the scores moved, and that its sensitivity, specificity and PPV differ from the validated ones. That share does not say how many ill outpatients are missed, 28 in 100 here, which only diagnoses count.

Predictions that need no diagnosis did worse than the recomputation given the true prevalence. Across the 174 transfers, the recomputation at a prevalence estimated from the site's unlabelled scores missed by a median of 13.7 points, the model's own mean probability among the flagged by 8.2, and that probability corrected to the estimated prevalence by 12.6. At Columbia, the estimate of the outpatients' prevalence from their unlabelled scores was 0.1%, against a true 27% among the outpatients it was read on.

### 3.7 Setting the threshold again on local labels

Set again on 100 outpatients with known diagnoses, the threshold caught 91.6% of the ill on average over 2,000 repetitions, against 71.5% with the inpatient threshold on the same patients (Figure 5). The 100 held 26 ill patients on average, between 13 and 41 across the repetitions. In 33.1% of the repetitions the new threshold still caught fewer than 90% of the ill outpatients it had not seen; for the mini-model and ECGFounder, 31.1% and 29.9%, about one repetition in three. The healthy paid: it flagged 70 of 100 healthy outpatients on average, against 29 with the inpatient threshold. The mini-model and ECGFounder behaved alike, catching 91.9% and 91.8% of the ill and flagging 72 and 70 of 100 healthy. With 200 outpatients, about 51 of them ill, the trained network caught 90.9%, flagged 66 of 100 healthy, and still fell short of 90% in 37.0% of the repetitions. More labelled outpatients narrowed the spread, the middle 80% of repetitions running from 83.8% to 98.0% with 100 and from 84.5% to 96.4% with 200, but did not lower the share that fell short: the rule places the threshold to catch 90% on average, with a margin above it that shrinks as the sample grows.

These 100 were drawn from the outpatients the threshold was read on. A clinic sets its threshold on past patients and reads it on new ones. EchoNext's validation group holds 858 other outpatients of the same hospital, 240 of them ill. Set on all of them, the threshold caught 86.3% of the ill test outpatients (81.7% to 89.9%), 87.8% (83.4% to 91.2%) for the mini-model and 89.7% (85.5% to 92.8%) for ECGFounder: short of 90% for all three, and for the trained network the interval excludes 90%. It flagged 52, 60 and 57 of 100 healthy outpatients. Set on 100 drawn from those 858, it caught 89.0%, 90.9% and 89.5% of the ill test outpatients on average and fell short of 90% in 38.4% to 48.5% of the repetitions. The untrained floor, set on the same 858, caught 91.9% and flagged 75 of 100 healthy.

The reset has a price in healthy patients flagged. In the same clinic of 1,000 outpatients, the new threshold would send 752 for an echocardiogram and miss 21 of the 256 ill, against 398 and 73 with the inpatient threshold: about 7 more echocardiograms for each extra ill outpatient found. With every outpatient's diagnosis known, catching 90 of 100 ill flags 59 of 100 healthy, so the threshold carried from the inpatients misses 18 more ill outpatients in 100 than one set for 90% among outpatients, and flags 30 fewer healthy ones. Most of the difference between 59 and 70 healthy flagged is the safety margin the rule takes on a small sample: it places the threshold slightly below the sample's own 90% point, so that it still catches 90% on average when the sample happens to be unrepresentative. Placed at the sample's own 90% point, with no margin, the threshold caught 88.8% on average, flagged 61 of 100 healthy, and fell short of 90% in 48.8% of the repetitions. The untrained floor, set again on 100 outpatients the same way, caught 91.6% and flagged 76 of 100 healthy: once the threshold is set on outpatients, the trained models flag 5 to 7 fewer healthy outpatients in 100 than a network that learnt nothing.

Net benefit puts the choice in one number for the trained network, per 1,000 outpatients (supplement, Table S7d). At a decision threshold of 10%, setting the threshold again on 100 outpatients nets 177, an echocardiogram for every outpatient 173, and the inpatient threshold 159. At 5%, an echocardiogram for everyone nets the most, 217 against 207. At 20%, the inpatient threshold does, 129 against 105.

### 3.8 Conformal prediction gave the plain percentile

Conformal prediction was the method this study set out with. It sets a threshold on a sample of patients with known diagnoses and guarantees that, on average over such samples, at least 90% of new ill patients are flagged, provided the new patients are exchangeable with the sample: drawn from the same population, so that their order carries no information [23]. Fitted within each diagnosis separately [24], its threshold for the ill is the 90% point of the ill sample with a small correction, the threshold of section 2.4; on the 1,013 ill calibration inpatients the correction changed little, the uncorrected 90th percentile catching 71.2% of ill outpatients against 71.6%. In practice it equals the plain percentile. Its guarantee did not hold for outpatients because they are not exchangeable with inpatients, and set again on 100 outpatients it held on average and fell short in about one repetition in three, as the guarantee allows. Its second threshold, set on the healthy, sends patients between the two thresholds to a human reader. That threshold catches no one the first misses: among outpatients it sent 36 ill and 27 healthy in 100 to a reader, against 36 and 49 among inpatients, and as a repair it lowered net benefit on average (Table 4). What a reader does with those patients was not measured.

## 4. Discussion

A PPV recomputed by Bayes' theorem at a clinic's true prevalence fell outside the observed PPV's interval in 75% of transfers, by a median of 5.3 points, too high in some and too low in others. On controls it missed by 0.3. The recomputation is right when only the prevalence changes, and between hospitals and care settings more than the prevalence changed.

Columbia shows how. The model did not get worse at telling ill from healthy outpatients; the threshold landed at a different point of the same curve. Ill and healthy outpatients both scored lower than their inpatient counterparts, so a fixed threshold caught fewer ill and flagged fewer healthy at once, and the PPV rose above what the inpatients' specificity predicted. A validation that reports the AUROC by care setting, as EchoNext's did [6], therefore does not show the sensitivity, specificity or PPV a clinic gets. Case mix explained about a quarter of the fall in sensitivity, and most of it happens among patients with the same findings, ejection fraction, age and sex. At Chongqing the error ran the other way, with specificity lower than at the source, so the direction of the miss cannot be guessed from the setting.

This is the spectrum effect [2,3], measured on the quantity a clinic acts on. It agrees with Leeflang and colleagues' finding that sensitivity and specificity move with prevalence [4], and with Attia and colleagues' low ejection fraction model, whose original cut-off caught 26.9% of ill patients in a Russian population sample where its AUROC was still 0.82 [9]. Carter and colleagues found performance holding across four sites chosen to represent the national population [10]. PRESENT-SHD kept its sensitivity at four other hospitals and in a population cohort [11], and it differs from this study in three ways. Its threshold was carried between hospitals and to a community cohort, not between the care settings of one hospital; it reports sensitivity at the carried threshold, not a recomputed PPV set against an observed one; and its model reads ECG images. One difference from both Carter's sites and PRESENT-SHD is the move here from hospital beds to clinics, where the ill carry fewer findings and the healthy score lower; this study does not test it against their sites. Methods for label shift, such as the maximum-likelihood correction of Saerens and colleagues [5,20], assume the ECGs within each diagnosis stay the same; at Columbia they did not, and the correction estimated a prevalence near zero. Han and Qu show that the ill patients a sample needs grow fast as the precision asked of a threshold tightens [25]; this study did not vary the ill and the ECGs apart, so it does not measure that.

A clinic adopting a model validated elsewhere therefore faces a choice the validation hid. It can keep the delivered threshold and measure the PPV it gives, or buy a sensitivity near 90% by setting the threshold again on its own patients, at the price of echocardiograms for most of its healthy outpatients, and check on new patients what it bought. Which is right depends on what a missed valve lesion or cardiomyopathy costs against an echocardiogram, which net benefit puts in one number at a stated decision threshold. The share of patients flagged costs nothing to watch, and at Columbia it fell below anything fewer ill patients could produce.

## 5. Limitations

- The structural heart disease result comes from one hospital system and two care settings. It has not been measured in a second hospital system, so its size there is not known.
- The three Columbia models learned from the same EchoNext patients, and the network trained here was trained once. The mini-model was trained by its authors and ECGFounder's network elsewhere, with only its final regression fitted here, so a quirk of one training run cannot explain the agreement of all three.
- Outpatients were told apart from inpatients only by the care setting EchoNext records, and they differ from them in recording years as well; the fall holds within each band of years. Why the ill outpatients score lower is explained only in part, the quarter that the case mix accounts for.
- The Columbia thresholds were set on validation inpatients. Set on every validation patient, the threshold caught 77 of 100 ill outpatients instead of 72: the size of the fall depends on where the threshold is set. Vendors pick their thresholds differently, and the PPV gap depends on where the threshold sits.
- The separate outpatients are one group of 858 from the same hospital; one group cannot say how much of its shortfall from 90% is chance.
- The healthy are defined with no time limit, and more healthy outpatients than inpatients were recorded over a year before their echocardiogram. Without them, the trained network flagged 32 of 100 healthy outpatients instead of 29, and the outpatient AUROC was 0.792; the specificity and the predictive values depend on this definition, the sensitivity does not.
- The intervals across transfers resample groups of transfers that share a model and a pair of populations; with four groups in each Columbia block, those intervals are rough.
- At Chongqing the definition of infarction, the population, the recording equipment and the era change together, and the design does not separate them. EchoNext's tracings carry no physical unit, and each target was standardised as its source was.
- The rotation models were trained on a few thousand ECGs each, fewer than a commercial model. The decision threshold of 10% for an echocardiogram is a stated assumption, not a measured preference, and net benefit counts the ill found and the healthy flagged, not outcomes or costs. What a reader does with a patient sent to them was not measured.

This is a retrospective measurement study on public, de-identified data. It is not a medical device and has no regulatory status, it involved no contact with patients, and nothing here is meant to guide the care of any patient.

## 6. What a clinic should do

- Count the PPV and the false alerts per ill patient found on its own patients with known diagnoses, at the threshold it will run, from a sample large enough that the interval is narrower than the margin of the decision. A PPV recomputed from a vendor's sensitivity and specificity can miss in either direction by more than that margin.
- Ask for a decision curve at a named decision threshold for the action the alert triggers, against treating everyone and treating no one. Among Columbia's outpatients the model did no better than an echocardiogram for everyone at 10%; at Shandong's prevalence the model as delivered did worse than doing nothing.
- Compare the share of patients flagged with the share at validation. A fall below what any prevalence allows means the scores moved, before any diagnosis is in.
- When a vendor offers a correction that needs no local diagnosis, ask for evidence that the clinic's healthy patients look like the vendor's. At Columbia's outpatient clinic they did not.
- When the threshold is set again on local patients, read it on patients it was not set on, and count what it costs in healthy patients flagged.
