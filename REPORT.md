# A 90% ECG threshold does not hold at another hospital or in another care setting

Ruben Abbou

PTB-XL · SPH · ACS-ECG · EchoNext · 45,923 tracings scored across three corpora for infarction, and 7,136 EchoNext ECGs for structural heart disease · draft of 5 October 2026

## Abstract

An ECG model scores each recording, and a cut-off on the score, the threshold, decides who is called ill. It is set to catch, say, 90% of the ill. A hospital adopting the model inherits it and sees one accuracy score, the AUROC, which does not say how many ill patients a given cut-off catches and can look fine while it misses them.

Cut-offs were moved unchanged and the ill caught counted: myocardial infarction, from PTB-XL in Germany to hospitals in Shandong and Chongqing; sinus rhythm, atrial fibrillation, left and right bundle-branch block and first-degree atrioventricular block, five collections taking turns; and structural heart disease, from 1,903 Columbia inpatients to 1,059 outpatients. Three ways of answering were compared: one cut-off saying yes or no to everyone, or a grey zone where the patient is referred to a doctor (conformal prediction), its edges set for 90% of all patients together, or for 90% of the ill and of the healthy separately.

Set for all patients together, the 90% held overall, not among the ill: 90.0% of PTB-XL patients got no wrong answer, yet 73.5% of infarctions were caught (flagged or referred). Set apart, it held where set; moved, it caught 84.0% of infarctions at Chongqing, 97.4% at Shandong, 87.7% across the five diagnoses, and 71.6% of ill outpatients against 90.4% of inpatients, while the AUROC barely moved, 0.815 to 0.805. Three differently built models lost alike. The share referred, visible without diagnoses, fell as misses rose.

Moved cut-offs did not keep their promise; a hundred local ECGs with known diagnoses restored it: infarctions caught at Chongqing rose to 93.8%, ill outpatients caught to 93.4% to 97.6% on average. A site should count how many of its own ill patients each cut-off catches, and reset it on about a hundred ECGs.

## 1. Introduction

A cut-off set to catch 90% of the ill at one hospital may catch fewer at another, and the accuracy score a hospital is shown does not reveal it. This section sets out why, what earlier work found, and what no study had measured.

An ECG model does not answer yes or no by itself. It gives each tracing, one recorded ECG, a score between 0 and 1, and a threshold, a cut-off on that score, turns the score into an answer: above it, the model calls the disease present. The cut-off is chosen on patients whose diagnoses are known, usually so that it catches a target share of the ill, such as 90%, a sensitivity of 90%. Choosing the cut-off on those patients is called calibration, and they are the calibration patients. The share it catches belongs to them. A hospital whose patients differ in how common or how severe the disease is, in the recording machines, or in how diagnoses are written down, can see the same cut-off catch a different share, and a yes-or-no answer gives no sign that this has happened.

What a receiving hospital is usually shown is the AUROC, the usual single accuracy score: the chance that a randomly chosen ill patient scores higher than a randomly chosen healthy one. It does not say how many ill patients a given cut-off catches. Two studies of one ECG model, which detects a weak heart pump (a low ejection fraction), show what follows. Applied to 4,277 adults of a Russian population study at the cut-off of its original study, the model kept an AUROC of 0.82 and caught 26.9% of the patients with a weak pump [19]; run at four US sites with a cut-off fixed in advance, it caught 84.5% of them and correctly cleared 83.6% of the others [20]. Whether a cut-off holds at a new site has to be measured each time.

Conformal prediction, a standard statistical method, adds a third answer. Instead of one cut-off it sets two. A patient whose score falls above the upper one is called ill, below the lower one healthy, and between them, in a grey zone, gets no machine answer: the patient is referred to a human, a doctor who reads the ECG. In the method's own terms, each patient receives the set of answers that cannot be ruled out, "ill", "healthy", or both, and a set holding both is a referral. The method promises a coverage: the share of patients whose set holds the right answer, meaning they get the right answer alone or are referred. Among the ill, coverage is the share caught, flagged as ill or referred; for a single cut-off, which refers no one, it is the sensitivity. The promise needs no assumption about the model, provided the cut-offs are set on calibration patients the model was not trained on [1].

Two limits of that promise shape this study. It holds only while the new patients resemble the calibration patients, in statistical terms drawn from the same population, and a change of hospital is exactly what breaks that. And it is an average over all patients together: 90% over everyone can hide a much lower share among the ill when the ill are few, because the healthy majority carries the average. Setting the two cut-offs for all patients together is called pooled calibration. The alternative sets each one for one group. The lower cut-off, below which a patient is told "healthy", is placed so that 90% of the ill calibration patients score above it; the upper cut-off, above which a patient is told "ill", so that 90% of the healthy score below it. This per-diagnosis calibration, known as class-conditional or Mondrian conformal prediction, keeps its promise within the ill and within the healthy whatever the share of ill patients [2]. Because each group has its own cut-off, a change in how common the disease is cannot by itself change the share of the ill it catches [2,4]. If that share still falls after a move, the ill patients themselves score differently. A third variant reweights the calibration patients toward the share of ill patients estimated at the new site [3,4]; it was run too, and Appendix H reports it.

Earlier work has shown pooled calibration failing the minority of ill patients at the site that set it. On a skin-lesion model, the 90% was met over all lesions while fewer than 90% of melanomas were caught [12]; two 2026 preprints report the same pattern, and its per-diagnosis repair, in drug screening and across fifteen tabular datasets [5,6]. A 2026 study of intensive-care arrhythmia detection from the pulse oximeter waveform met the 90% over all patients (90.0%) while catching 7.5% of critical rhythms, raised to 82.5% by per-diagnosis calibration [14]. On the ECG, a conference abstract applies conformal prediction to acute infarction on PTB-XL alone, reporting an AUROC of 0.923 without it against 0.932 and 0.943 with its pooled and per-diagnosis forms, and a share of all patients given no wrong answer of 84.8% and 86.7%; it reports nothing within each diagnosis, and sets no single cut-off beside the conformal ones [7]. El Allam and Hamlich compared pooled with per-diagnosis calibration on PTB-XL, normal against infarction: "pooled calibration achieved 91.64% Normal coverage and 88.59% MI coverage; Mondrian calibration achieved 90.65% and 90.53%, reducing the class-coverage gap from 3.06 to 0.12 percentage points" [15]. The gap at the site that set the cut-off, and its repair, are therefore theirs first on the ECG. They did not move the cut-off to another site, and say so: their second dataset "should not be interpreted as evidence of cross-database transfer".

For structural heart disease the move that matters stays inside one hospital. A model that detects it from the ECG learns from patients who had an echocardiogram, mostly inpatients, and a screening programme would run it on outpatients. EchoNext, trained on more than a million ECGs from eight New York hospitals, is the reference model for that task, and both of its papers find its AUROC stable across care settings: 84.3% among outpatients and 84.1% among inpatients and in the emergency department for the full model [17], and no care setting significantly different from the whole test set for the public mini-model [18]. Neither reports what a cut-off fixed in one setting catches in another. Carried to another hospital's records (MIMIC-IV), the mini-model's AUROC was 0.790 against 0.820 on its own test patients, and its risks were overestimated [21]; in a community cohort aged 65 to 85 the full model's AUROC fell, which its authors attribute to milder disease [22]. A per-diagnosis cut-off need not hold when more than the share of ill patients changes: in multiple sclerosis imaging, one set on 3 T scanners for 90% covered 85.8% of patients at 3 T and 77.5% at 1.5 T [23].

No study measures, for the ECG, how many of the ill a cut-off catches after it has been carried to another population without being reset; none sets a single cut-off beside the conformal ones in the error terms a clinician uses, and none reports how many patients each option refers. Searches of the ECG literature on 2 and 4 September 2026, repeated on 8 September across PubMed, arXiv, Crossref, OpenAlex, Google Scholar and OpenReview, found none. This study asks one question twice: when a cut-off set to catch 90% at one place is used at another, how many of the ill does it catch, for each diagnosis, and does anything a site can see without knowing the diagnoses show the change? The first test moves infarction cut-offs between hospitals, from PTB-XL to Shandong and Chongqing, and widens that to five collections taking turns at setting cut-offs for five other diagnoses. The second keeps the hospital and the definition of the disease, and moves structural heart disease cut-offs from Columbia's inpatients to its outpatients. At Chongqing the definition of an infarction changes with the patients; at Columbia only the patients change, and reading the two moves together rests on that difference. Every technique applied here is established; the study adds the measurement.

## 2. Methods

ECGs with known diagnoses were split into two groups: on the first the cut-offs were set, on the second they were used unchanged, and the ill patients caught were counted. No model was retrained for the move.

### 2.1 Two moves and their data

For infarction, three public collections of ECGs (corpora) were used. The site where the cut-offs are set is called the source, and a site that receives them unchanged the target. Cut-offs were set on PTB-XL; the two Chinese hospitals received them, each scored once, and no diagnosis from either reached any cut-off. Appendix A accounts for every record.

| Dataset | Country, years | Tracings scored | Share with infarction | Role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PTB-XL [9] | Germany, 1989–96 | 2,198 | 25.0% | source; also tested on its own tracings kept aside |
| SPH, Shandong [10] | China, 2019–20 | 25,770 | 1.0% | target |
| ACS-ECG, Chongqing [11] | China, 2015–24 | 17,955 | 14.9% | target |

Infarction is present in 25.0% of PTB-XL's tracings, 1.0% of Shandong's and 14.9% of Chongqing's, and the three define it differently. At Chongqing a tracing counts as infarction when the discharge diagnosis names an acute myocardial infarction, in a cohort whose every patient underwent coronary angiography; PTB-XL records what the ECG shows, mostly older infarct patterns. Shandong codes infarction as acute, recent or old, and 89.6% of its infarctions are old, so its definition sits closer to PTB-XL's, though the remaining 10.4% are acute, recent or unspecified. The Shandong move is therefore the nearer of the two to a change in how common infarction is and nothing else, while the Chongqing move also changes what counts as an infarction.

For structural heart disease, EchoNext v1.1.1 holds 100,000 ECGs from Columbia, each recorded within a year before a transthoracic echocardiogram. The diagnosis studied is moderate or worse structural heart disease on that echocardiogram, a composite of eleven findings [18]. The cut-offs are set on the 1,903 inpatient ECGs of the dataset's validation part and used unchanged on its test part: 1,059 outpatient ECGs, and the emergency and inpatient ECGs beside them. Each patient contributes one ECG, and no patient appears in two roles (training, setting the cut-offs, testing).

| Cohort | ECGs, one per patient | Structural heart disease | Ejection fraction 45% or less | Role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Validation part, inpatients | 1,903 | 53.2% | 24.5% | sets the cut-offs |
| Test part, inpatients | 2,203 | 52.5% | 24.4% | receives them, same setting |
| Test part, emergency | 1,971 | 40.0% | 15.7% | receives them |
| Test part, outpatients | 1,059 | 25.6% | 6.6% | receives them |

Unlike infarction, the diagnosis does not change between these cohorts: every one is read from echocardiograms at the same hospital under the same definitions, so what changes is how many patients are ill and which patients they are.

### 2.2 Models

For infarction, a standard deep network for ECGs (a one-dimensional residual network, or ResNet) was trained on PTB-XL, which comes divided into ten parts (folds): it learned on the first eight and was tested on fold 10, where its AUROC for infarction is 0.932, 95% interval 0.921 to 0.943. It was then left unchanged, so every option below works on identical scores and any difference between them comes from where the cut-offs sit. It is an ordinary model, not a contribution; Appendix B describes it.

For structural heart disease, four models scored every ECG. The study's residual network, trained on the 72,475 ECGs of EchoNext's training part, reaches an AUROC of 0.834 on the whole test part. The published EchoNext mini-model, run on its authors' weights with nothing refitted, reaches 0.820, against the 82.0% its authors report [18]. ECGFounder, a model pre-trained on more than ten million ECGs from another hospital [24], was left unchanged (frozen) and read through one simple regression per finding (a probe) fitted on the training part; it reaches 0.824. The study's network left untrained, with random weights (random initialisation), read through the same probes, is the floor any pre-trained model has to clear; it reaches 0.792. Appendix C gives how each was run.

### 2.3 Three ways of answering a patient

Each model gives every ECG a score, and the scores never change; the three options differ only in where they place the cut-offs and in what they do with a patient whose score falls near them.

The first option, a single cut-off, answers yes or no for every patient. It is placed so that 90% of the ill calibration patients score above it, a sensitivity of 90%, and stands for current practice.

The second and third options are the two forms of conformal prediction described in section 1. Each sets two cut-offs and refers every patient whose score falls between them to a doctor. The second sets both cut-offs for all calibration patients together, so that 90% of them, ill and healthy pooled, are given no wrong answer: pooled calibration. The third sets each cut-off for one group, the lower so that 90% of the ill calibration patients are not told they are healthy and the upper so that 90% of the healthy are not told they are ill: per-diagnosis calibration. Setting the cut-offs for all patients or for each diagnosis is the only difference between the second and third options.

The single cut-off and the per-diagnosis option catch exactly the same ill patients, by construction: the per-diagnosis option's lower cut-off sits where the single cut-off sits. What the per-diagnosis option adds is its upper cut-off, which turns some of the healthy patients the single cut-off would have flagged into referrals. The pooled option matches neither. Appendix H gives how the cut-offs are computed.

Every patient ends in one of three places: the right answer alone, a referral, or the wrong answer alone. An ill patient is recognised, referred or missed; a healthy patient is cleared, referred or falsely flagged. Each share is of all the ill, or of all the healthy, so a referred patient counts as neither right nor wrong and the options can be compared whether they refer or not. Coverage of the ill is the recognised and the referred together, and nothing below counts a referred patient as recognised.

### 2.4 Evaluation

Every figure was read on patients who played no part in setting the cut-offs, and repeated over many random splits so that no single split carries a result.

For infarction, PTB-XL's test fold was split in half by patient 200 times (200 splits, or draws); each time one half set the cut-offs, and the other half and the two Chinese hospitals received them. Each infarction figure is the average over those 200 splits. Appendix H gives how the intervals on differences were computed.

To see what a site could notice without knowing the diagnoses, five recording faults were applied in software to PTB-XL's test fold and the tracings scored again, with the cut-offs set on clean tracings: the arm electrodes swapped; every lead's amplitude scaled by 0.8 and by 1.25, as a machine calibrated to the wrong standard would record; a slow 0.3 Hz drift of 0.5 mV added, as breathing and electrode movement would; and every lead's polarity inverted. Appendix I describes how they were scored.

For structural heart disease, a share carries a 95% confidence interval (Wilson's method), and an AUROC a 95% interval from 500 resamplings of the patients. Whether milder disease explains the loss was tested by a rule fixed before any result by severity was computed: the ill outpatients were reweighted to look like the ill inpatients in six groups (one finding, or two and more; ejection fraction 35 or less, 36 to 45, above 45%), with intervals from 2,000 resamplings. Severity would explain all of the loss if the reweighted figure reached 90%, and none of it if it did not differ from the observed one.

To measure what local diagnoses repair, the receiving site's patients were split once by patient into two halves. The cut-offs were reset on a growing number of ECGs from the first half, and read on the second. At Chongqing the second half holds 8,984 tracings, and the first gave from none to 2,000 ECGs, used alone or added to PTB-XL's calibration patients. At Columbia the second half holds 530 outpatients, 137 of them ill, and the first gave 25 to 400 ECGs, drawn 200 times, each draw counting the ill patients it holds.

### 2.5 Five corpora in the calibration role

One collection setting cut-offs and two receiving them cannot tell a general effect of moving cut-offs from something particular to PTB-XL and Chongqing. Five collections therefore took turns at setting the cut-offs: PTB-XL, Shandong, Chapman-Shaoxing with Ningbo, Georgia, and CPSC 2018 with its extension, the last three from the PhysioNet/CinC Challenge 2021 [25]. Each was trained and calibrated on its own ECGs, and its cut-offs used once, unchanged, on each of the other four, giving twenty ordered source-target pairs, and 92 away cells once the diagnoses are counted separately (one cell is one collection's cut-off for one diagnosis, read at another collection).

Infarction is not recorded across those collections, so the rotation runs on five diagnoses that are: sinus rhythm, atrial fibrillation, left bundle-branch block, right bundle-branch block and first-degree atrioventricular block. Each is a yes-or-no question on its own, so the share caught is the same quantity the infarction study reports. Shandong's "Normal ECG" describes a whole tracing rather than the rhythm, so sinus rhythm is left out on Shandong and carries twelve pairs against twenty for the others. Appendix D gives how the diagnoses were matched across collections, the sizes used and the screen for tracings filed twice.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Where the threshold was set

Where the cut-offs were set, the per-diagnosis option caught 90% of the ill, and the pooled option did not: it met its 90% over all patients by serving the healthy majority. Figure 1 shows why. The pooled option places its cut-offs well to the right of the single cut-off, because three quarters of PTB-XL's calibration patients have no infarction and the 90% is asked over everyone, so answering the majority well is the cheapest way to meet it. The per-diagnosis option cannot make that trade, since each of its cut-offs answers only to its own group.

Dashed lines mark the cut-offs; the labelled bands show the answer given in each range of scores. In the figure, "class-conditional" is the per-diagnosis option, "deferred" is referred, and "MI" is myocardial infarction.

The pooled option gave no wrong answer to 90.0% of all patients and caught 73.5% of infarctions, telling 26.5% of them they had none, against 9.9% under the single cut-off (Table 1, Figure 2). Its promise was kept, since it covers the average patient and not the ill. The per-diagnosis option caught 90.1% within each diagnosis.

Whether the per-diagnosis option looks better or worse depends on what it is compared with. Against the single cut-off, it missed the same infarctions, about 10%, and lowered false alarms from 19.7% to 9.9% of patients without infarction. It did so by referring them, not by getting them right: the share correctly cleared stayed at 80.3% under both, and it referred 9.7% of patients without infarction and 12.8% of those with it. Against the pooled option, it raised false alarms from 4.5% to 9.9% and cut missed infarctions from 26.5% to 9.9%. Counting the missed and the referred together, 22.7% of infarctions were never told "infarction" by the per-diagnosis option, 36.7% by the pooled option, and 9.9% by the single cut-off.

The same gap appears in the five rotation collections, so it is not PTB-XL's. Reading their own held-out tracings with pooled cut-offs, they cover 90.1% of all cases and 29.0% of the cases carrying the diagnosis they are asked about, averaged over the 24 home cells (a collection reading its own tracings for one diagnosis): the 90% is met over everyone and missed by sixty-one points among the ill. The gap is wider than for infarction because in 23 of the 24 cells the diagnosis is rarer than infarction was, present in a median 4.3% of tracings, and cut-offs set for everyone follow the majority. The exception is sinus rhythm on PTB-XL, present in 82.7% and caught at 93.2%. The per-diagnosis option closes the gap at home on all five collections, covering 92.0% of the cases carrying the diagnosis against 90.2% of all cases.

At Columbia the per-diagnosis cut-offs, read on test inpatients of the setting that set them, caught 90.4% of the ill for the trained network, and 89.5% to 90.7% for the three models with the highest AUROC. Where the cut-offs were set, the per-diagnosis option delivers what it was asked for, in both studies.

### 3.2 Carried to other hospitals

Moved unchanged, the infarction cut-offs caught fewer infarctions at Chongqing and more at Shandong, and across the rotation how much a moved cut-off lost depended mostly on which collection had set it.

At Shandong, the pooled option caught 93.6% of infarctions, above the 90% asked for: with the cut-off fixed, the share caught depends only on how the ill score, and Shandong's infarctions score higher than PTB-XL's. The healthy did better there too, 94.8% not falsely flagged, and the per-diagnosis option, applied where nothing needed repair, raised the infarctions caught to 97.4%. At Chongqing, the pooled option caught 72.5% of infarctions, 17.5 points short of the target, and the per-diagnosis option 84.0%, still short. Patients without infarction lost more than those with it: the share not falsely flagged fell from 95.5% at PTB-XL to 78.0% under the pooled option and 69.4% under the per-diagnosis option, so whatever changed at Chongqing did not act on the ill alone.

In the terms a clinician uses, the single cut-off set for 90% sensitivity caught 84.0% of infarctions at Chongqing and 97.4% at Shandong. The per-diagnosis option catches the same infarctions; of its 84.0% at Chongqing, 74.6% were told "infarction" and 9.5% were referred; at Shandong, 94.9% and 2.4%. The single cut-off's specificity fell from 80.3% at PTB-XL to 57.2% at Chongqing. On patients without infarction the per-diagnosis option did worse than the pooled one at Chongqing: it flagged 30.6% of them as infarction and referred 12.2%, where the pooled option flagged 22.1% and referred 6.1%. The fourth option, which reweights the calibration patients toward the share of infarction it estimates at the new hospital, misjudged that share at both hospitals and caught no more than the per-diagnosis option (Appendix H).

Across the rotation, moved cut-offs also lost or gained, and which collection set them mattered more than the average. Under the pooled option, the share given no wrong answer and the share of the ill caught fall together, to 82.3% and 23.3%, which is the shift of moving, added to a gap that was already there. The per-diagnosis option, which closes that gap at home, lost part of it once moved: on average it reads 87.7% within the diagnosis, against 90% asked. The worst single move loses 41.5 points, CPSC's sinus-rhythm cut-off used on PTB-XL. The opposite directions at Shandong and Chongqing were therefore not chance variation around one stable loss, and two hospitals alone could not have shown that. ### 3.3 Carried to the outpatients of the same hospital

Moved from Columbia's inpatients to its outpatients, the per-diagnosis cut-offs asked to catch 90% of the ill caught about 72%, for every one of the three strongest models.

The per-diagnosis cut-offs set on inpatients caught 71.6% of the outpatients who have moderate or worse structural heart disease on echocardiography, did not falsely flag 98.1% of those who do not, and referred 29.4% of outpatients to a doctor. In the emergency department they caught 86.2% of the ill, and among test inpatients, with the same cut-offs, 90.4%. An ejection fraction of 45% or less was caught more often: 87.1% of those outpatients. The pooled option caught 74.5% of the ill outpatients, and none of them for three rarer findings (aortic stenosis, aortic regurgitation, pericardial effusion), whose pooled cut-offs the healthy majority sets.

The loss is not one model's. The three models with the highest AUROC lost the same: 71.6% of the ill outpatients caught for the residual network and for ECGFounder, 72.7% for the published mini-model (Table 2). A network trained here, one trained by the EchoNext authors and one pre-trained elsewhere all fall to the same level, which points at the outpatients' ECGs rather than at one model; the three were fitted to the same training patients, so they are not three independent tests. The untrained floor model loses less because it refers more outpatients.

### 3.4 What a site sees without knowing the diagnoses

A site running a model without fresh diagnoses can watch two figures: the AUROC it was shown at adoption, and the share of patients referred. Neither showed the loss.

At Columbia, the AUROC did not show it. Among test inpatients the trained network's AUROC was 0.815 and among outpatients 0.805, with overlapping intervals for every model, while the share of the ill it caught fell from 90.4% to 71.6% (Figure 5). The single cut-off moved the other way on the healthy, its specificity rising from 38.8%–42.8% among test inpatients to 71.1%–71.3% among outpatients for the three models. The cut-off now sat at a different point of an almost unchanged trade-off between sensitivity and specificity, so a report of AUROC by care setting reads as a success. With 25.6% of outpatients ill, a positive answer from the trained network was right for 46.0% of the outpatients it flagged, where the inpatients' sensitivity and specificity would have predicted 34.2%.

### 3.5 Why the coverage fell

The ill patients scored differently at the receiving site, and the two moves point at different reasons: at Chongqing what counts as an infarction changed along with everything else, at Columbia part of the loss is milder disease.

Because the per-diagnosis option sets the cut-off for the ill on the ill alone, a change in how common the disease is cannot by itself lower the share caught; where it still fell, the ill patients themselves changed. At Chongqing, an infarction is a discharge diagnosis of acute infarction rather than an ECG finding, and the scores of both the ill and the healthy sit elsewhere than at PTB-XL, those of the healthy having moved further. The definition, the population, the recording machines and the years differ together between PTB-XL and Chongqing, and this design measures the result without separating them.

At Columbia the definition is the same. Severity explains about a third of the lost coverage. The ill outpatients are ill more mildly than the ill inpatients who set the cut-offs: they carry fewer of the eleven findings, keep a higher ejection fraction, and have less right ventricular dysfunction, tricuspid regurgitation and pericardial effusion, with aortic stenosis the exception (Table F1). With the inpatient cut-offs, outpatients with two findings or more are caught more often than those with one, and those with an ejection fraction of 45% or less more often than those above it (Table 5).

### 3.6 What repaired it: the receiving site's own labels

In both moves, a hundred ECGs with known diagnoses from the receiving site did what no option and no model did: reset on them, the cut-offs caught close to 90% of the ill again.

At Chongqing, with PTB-XL's cut-offs, the pooled option reads 73.7% with no target records, read on the 8,984 tracings kept for evaluation (72.5% over the whole cohort). Reset on 100 Chongqing tracings, it takes it to 88.9%, where 500 and 2,000 leave it at 88.8% and 88.6% (Figure 6). Adding the same tracings to PTB-XL's calibration patients instead reaches 74.8%, 79.3% and 84.2% over the same steps and has not caught up at 2,000: what repairs the cut-off is being set on the receiving site's patients, not the number of patients it is set on. ## 4. Discussion

A cut-off set to catch 90% of the ill at one place caught a different share at another, in both moves, because the ill patients scored differently there; a hundred local ECGs with known diagnoses brought it back, and nothing a site sees without diagnoses warned of the loss.

Across hospitals the infarction cut-off missed its target at both sites, below it at Chongqing and above it at Shandong, and five collections taking turns showed the direction and size of the change to depend on which collection set the cut-off more than on any average. Inside one hospital, where the definition of the disease stayed fixed, the per-diagnosis cut-off that caught 90.4% of ill inpatients caught 71.6% of ill outpatients, and three models built in different ways lost the same. The receiving site's own ECGs repaired the loss in both: a hundred brought the share of the ill caught to within about a point of 90% or above it on average, and one draw in ten still fell below 90% on EchoNext.

The figure a receiving site is shown does not answer that question. The EchoNext authors read a stable AUROC across care settings as a stable model [17,18]; on the same data the AUROC held while the share of the ill caught fell, because the cut-off moved along an almost unchanged trade-off between sensitivity and specificity. Between hospitals the AUROC did move with the share caught, and still said nothing of how far: at Chongqing the single cut-off lost six points of sensitivity and twenty-three of specificity. Published ECG studies show the same: an AUROC of 0.82 kept in a Russian population came with a sensitivity of 26.9% [19], so only the cut-off's own figures showed the loss. The EchoNext authors' community study attributes its fall in AUROC to milder disease [22], the change that severity partly measures here.

Where the cut-offs were set, a 90% asked of all patients together was compatible with failing many of the ill: 90.0% of all patients against 73.5% of infarctions, in a cohort where 25% have one. That reproduces the effect reported on skin lesions [12], in drug screening and tabular data [5,6], and on the ECG by El Allam and Hamlich, who closed a 3.06-point gap between normal and infarction to 0.12 by setting the cut-offs per diagnosis [15]. The gap is larger here because the task is harder, infarction against everything else at 25% rather than infarction against normal at 36.4%. A single figure over all patients says close to nothing about the ill when they are few, and reporting it per diagnosis is the minimum for such a tool.

Most of the benefit does not need conformal prediction. A rule from 1970 that refers the patients between two plain cut-offs, each at the 90th percentile of one diagnosis's scores, reproduces the per-diagnosis results at all three infarction sites [16]: the infarctions caught within 0.47, 0.04 and 0.34 points, the patients without infarction not falsely flagged within 0.16, 0.13 and 0.25, while referring under one percentage point fewer patients. What does the work is setting a cut-off for each diagnosis separately. Conformal prediction adds a small correction for the size of the calibration group, worth 0.47 of a point at PTB-XL, and a name and a target for what a receiving site should measure. Appendix E gives the same comparison across the rotation, where the correction counts for more because the ill calibration patients are fewer.

Against current practice, the per-diagnosis option offers fewer false alarms at the same number of missed patients, and the false alarms it removes become referrals, not correct answers: a doctor reads roughly one ECG in ten in exchange for ten points fewer automated false alarms at PTB-XL, and at Columbia 29.4% to 32.3% of outpatients are referred to bring false alarms down to 1.8% to 2.3%. Whether that trade is worth it turns on the cost of a missed diagnosis, an unnecessary workup and a doctor's read, none of which this study evaluates. Whether clinicians decide better when shown "ill or healthy, unsure" than when shown one answer was tested by a reader study in which 62 cardiologists read 20 ECG cases: they were more accurate with set answers, with an effect size (Cohen's d) of 0.53 on complex cases and 0.67 among cardiologists with under ten years' experience, and gained further from a written explanation (d = 0.62). It was designed to show the sets were no worse, against a margin of -0.20, so it says a referral route is tolerable, not what it costs [8].

The two moves lost coverage for different reasons. At Chongqing the definition of an infarction moved with the patients, and this design cannot take the gap apart; at Shandong, the move nearest to a change in how common infarction is and nothing else, nothing failed. A loss after a move between hospitals is therefore shown at Chongqing alone, the one site whose change the design cannot take apart. The move from inpatients to outpatients supplies what that design lacks: the definition stayed the same and the share caught still fell. Milder disease accounts for about a third of the fall, and the rest sits within severity groups: of the ill outpatients whose ejection fraction is above 45%, 64.7% to 66.7% are caught, against 83.5% to 84.6% of the calibration inpatients in the same band. What else sets them apart, the type of finding, age, a coexisting rhythm or something in the ECG the diagnosis does not record, is not measured here, and six groups probably understate what severity explains [belief: coarse groups leave severity differences inside each one].

What a site adopting such a model should do follows from both moves. It should count, on its own patients, the share of the ill each cut-off catches, for each diagnosis, since neither the figure over all patients nor the AUROC carries it. Counting it needs ill patients more than tracings: pinning the share caught to within two points takes 865 infarctions, which is 5,798 tracings at Chongqing's share of infarction and 85,735 at Shandong's. To reset the cut-off rather than to certify it, about a hundred ECGs with known diagnoses from the receiving site moved the cut-off most of the way in both moves, at the price of more false alarms: the share of the healthy not falsely flagged fell from 97.5% to 90.5% for the trained network at Columbia. Twenty-five did not hold, and a hundred outpatients hold only a couple of dozen ill. Neither the share referred nor the AUROC replaces diagnoses: at Columbia both stayed quiet while the share caught fell. And a site should expect its own result to differ from any one published move, since across the rotation results spread by up to 13 points (one standard deviation) depending on which collection set the cut-off.

## 5. Limitations

The study counts missed and referred patients, not what happens to them, and it rests on few moves. Each limit below says what a reader should not conclude.

No clinical outcome was measured. A referral is a rate, not a benefit: whether sending one ECG in ten to a doctor improves care depends on who reads it and how fast, neither of which was measured. At Columbia the per-diagnosis option refers 29.4% of outpatients and 42.3% of test inpatients for the trained network, and whether a clinic can absorb that was not measured either.

The moves are few. For infarction, two receiving hospitals, one diagnosis and one collection setting the cut-offs, with the two hospitals moving in opposite directions, so neither the direction nor the size of the effect carries to a third site. The rotation widens that to five collections and twenty pairs, on other diagnoses. The EchoNext move stays inside one hospital, whose ECGs all come from one manufacturer's machines.

Between hospitals, causes are mixed. Recording machines, population, years and the definition of the disease differ together between PTB-XL and the receiving hospitals, so the design measures the resulting change without attributing it to any one of them.

The promise is an average. The share caught is an average over the patients with a diagnosis and says nothing about one tracing, and the per-diagnosis promise depends on the patient's true diagnosis, which is unknown when the ECG is read.

The infarction figures carry a spread, not a confidence interval. The variation quoted with them is how much the result changes from one split of PTB-XL to another, with the receiving hospitals held fixed, so it is smaller than a confidence interval. At Shandong it is 0.34 points, while the hospital's own 260 infarctions give a 95% Wilson interval of 89.8% to 95.9%, four points below the estimate and two above. The 90% asked for sits inside that interval, so the 93.6% there is not evidence of catching more than asked. Resampling the cohorts as well leaves the differences this study rests on intact, the infarctions caught by the per-diagnosis option over the pooled one: +16.7 points at PTB-XL, 95% interval 11.1 to 23.2, and +11.5 at Chongqing, 8.8 to 14.6. It widens the intervals on each option's own level to 66.5% to 80.1% for pooled and 84.6% to 94.9% for per-diagnosis, which remain apart.

No difference by sex or age is established for the per-diagnosis option. Appendix G gives the share caught by sex and age. Under the per-diagnosis option, the only difference at PTB-XL whose interval excludes zero is a fall with age among patients without infarction, on 253 women with infarction and 232 infarctions aged 75 and over. Sex and age are the two groups PTB-XL records; the receiving hospitals publish nothing that would allow the same breakdown. At Columbia the share of ill outpatients caught is 61.1% (52.4% to 69.2%) for women and 80.7% (73.5% to 86.3%) for men, an exploratory reading with no correction for multiple comparisons.

One scoring rule and one target were used throughout: 90%, with the most common way of turning the model's probability into a conformal score. Appendix H gives what a second rule and an 80% target change. The choice of 90% is conventional and not defended against any particular clinical cost.

The share caught is not the predictive value. It is not the chance that a flagged patient has the disease, which depends directly on how common the disease is: the per-diagnosis option's positive predictive value for infarction is 9.2% at Shandong, 29.9% at Chongqing and 72.4% at PTB-XL, in `results/auxiliary.json`.

The faults were applied in software, at PTB-XL only, one at a time and at one strength each. A real site's faults reach some tracings, in combination, at strengths nobody chose. They show that the share referred can stay flat while the share caught collapses, not how often that happens.

A cut-off can be impossible to set when a diagnosis has few calibration patients; every patient is then flagged or referred, which looks like a high share caught. Under the per-diagnosis option three source-diagnosis pairs of section 3.2 do this; under the reweighted option, which sets its cut-offs within each diagnosis and then reweights, fifteen do. The per-diagnosis figures there are given with those pairs and without (Appendix E), and no average of the reweighted option is quoted. At Columbia, resetting the cut-offs on fewer than 100 ECGs meets the same limit.

Each rotation collection, and the EchoNext ResNet, was trained once, from one random starting point, so the spread across collections and between EchoNext models also carries the variation of a single training run.

Three of the five networks compared for infarction in Appendix E were pre-trained on collections used here, and their published descriptions are the only record of that, so they cannot be ranked against each other and no result rests on them.

Severity is only partly measured. Six groups of findings and ejection fraction explain 29% to 35% of the EchoNext loss. They do not describe all that distinguishes an ill outpatient, and the comparison within each band is against the ECGs that set the cut-off.

## Appendix C. Running the EchoNext arms

The mini-model's weights come from the authors' IntroECG repository (commit 15233e93) and hold tensors only, so `torch.load` reads them with `weights_only=True`. Its architecture is `EchoNextMini` in `src/ecs/echonext_mini.py`, written from the shapes of the checkpoint rather than copied, since the repository states no licence; every tensor of the checkpoint loads into it, with none missing. It reads the tracing as distributed and the seven tabular features the distribution ships already standardised (sex, age, rates and intervals), which the other three arms do not see. Scoring the validation and test splits took 2.9 seconds on the Apple GPU.

ECGFounder's checkpoint holds one numpy scalar beside its tensors, which `weights_only=True` refuses on torch 2.2.2. It is read through an unpickler that resolves five names (the tensor rebuild, an ordered dict, the numpy scalar, its dtype and a byte codec) and refuses any other, so the file is never unpickled in full. Its network is the authors' Net1D, vendored in `third_party/ecgfounder`. The model card asks for 500 Hz, so each tracing is resampled from 250 Hz by polyphase filtering; the amplitude stays in z-score, since no millivolt scale exists to restore. The 1,024 features before the dense head feed one logistic probe per label. Embedding the 82,543 training, validation and test ECGs and fitting the probes took 332 seconds.

EchoNext's tracings have no physical unit. Each lead was median-filtered, clipped at its 0.1st and 99.9th percentiles and standardised with a mean and a standard deviation computed over the training set, so every tracing here is in z-score at 250 Hz, as `results/echonext_provenance.json` records for all 100,000 of them. The authors' code package publishes those per-lead means and standard deviations in the integer counts of the GE MUSE export, without stating how many microvolts a count is, so the millivolt scale cannot be recovered. Moving a model trained here to another hospital means replaying the same preprocessing on that hospital's ECGs, with that hospital's own standardisation, which absorbs part of any difference between electrocardiographs.

Screening the first ten seconds of the twelve canonical leads, quantised to ten microvolts, found 485 groups of identical tracings sitting in two parts a figure is read across, 421 of them in CPSC, 56 in Georgia and 8 in Chapman-Shaoxing with Ningbo, some straddling the boundary between training and test. ## Appendix E. The rotation in detail: per diagnosis, starved thresholds, draw spread and the plain reject rule

In this appendix a pair is one collection's cut-off for one diagnosis (the source) read at another collection (the target), "away" is such a move, and "home" a collection reading its own held-out tracings. The bias of a pair is the share of the diagnosis it catches minus the 90% asked, as a fraction.

Both per-diagnosis figures of section 3.2, at home and away, include the three source-diagnosis pairs that cover by declining to answer; over the pairs whose per-class threshold was finite, home and away read 91.0% and 86.1%, and it is the second figure that describes the scheme answering rather than abstaining. Per diagnosis the mean away bias is −0.008 for atrial fibrillation over twenty pairs, −0.039 for right bundle-branch block over twenty, and −0.102 for sinus rhythm over the twelve pairs that class carries. The spread across sources is larger than any mean: its standard deviation runs from 0.058 on first-degree atrioventricular block to 0.130 on sinus rhythm. After the worst pair of section 3.2, PTB-XL's left bundle-branch block threshold spent on CPSC comes next at 34.7 points. Both of those thresholds were finite in every draw. At home the scheme sits within 0.1 points of the target on sinus rhythm and over-covers by 1.8 to 3.0 points on the other four.

A pooled threshold is a quantile of the whole calibration sample and is finite in every one of the 140 cells the grid holds at this setting: 92 away readings, 24 by a corpus on its own held-out records, and 24 on the calibration part held back from fitting, so no pooled coverage in the rotation comes from abstaining. A per-label threshold can starve.

On Shandong's left bundle-branch block the per-label threshold is infinite in 189 of 200 draws, on its first-degree atrioventricular block in 152, and on Chapman-Shaoxing with Ningbo's left bundle-branch block in 132: too few calibration cases of the label for the 90th percentile of their scores to fall below the maximum, so the rule admits both labels and coverage reads high without the scheme separating them. Their sets average 1.49 to 1.76 labels of a possible two, against 1.015 on the other eighty away pairs, and their coverage reads 0.970 to 1.000. Removing them moves left bundle-branch block from +0.004 over twenty pairs to −0.053 over the twelve where the threshold was finite, a change of sign, and first-degree atrioventricular block from −0.002 to −0.022. Both columns are in `results/rotation.json`.

The spread over calibration draws and the bootstrap over the target cohort are different quantities, and the rotation shows which dominates. A bootstrap width is a 95% interval and a draw spread a standard deviation, so the second is put on the first's scale, and the two corrections are read apart. Over the 92 source-target-diagnosis pairs of `results/rotation_uncertainty.csv`, per-label calibration has a median bootstrap width of 0.055 against a draw spread of 0.122 on the same scale, the draw being the wider on 78 of the 92; pooled calibration inverts it, at 0.041 against 0.036, the draw being the wider on 38. The per-label draw moves more because its threshold is a quantile of one label's calibration cases, the smallest sample in the design. One quantity is what the calibration sample could have been and the other what the cohort could have been, and a receiving site has to read both. 248 of the file's 1,624 rows rest on fewer than 25 cases of the diagnosis and are flagged rather than dropped.

Chow's rule places one empirical quantile per class, so the per-label column is the like-for-like comparison, and over its 92 away pairs the two constructions differ by a median of 0.026 in coverage and by as much as 0.365. At the source the difference is 0.47 of a coverage point, the size of the finite-sample correction, and the rule is the per-label construction minus that correction, so the difference is the correction itself. Across five corpora it is about five times the source-site figure, because the per-class calibration samples are smaller than PTB-XL's 275 infarctions, and every departure above 0.35 sits on the three pairs whose conformal threshold is infinite, where the (n+1) rank runs past the largest score and the plain quantile does not. Per-label tuning still does most of the work, the conformal apparatus still adds only the correction and the vocabulary, and the correction counts for more where a label is rare.

Over the rotation the plain reject rule of section 4 sits further from the per-diagnosis scheme: the correction is worth a median of 2.6 points, about five times what it is worth at the source, because the rotation's per-class samples are smaller. A symmetric reject rule, one that refuses a band around a single threshold, does not reproduce the infarction results: widened until it refuses as much as the per-diagnosis scheme, it takes infarction coverage at the source to 93.2% and non-infarction coverage to 85.2%, and at Chongqing reads 86.9% and 61.9% against 84.0% and 69.4%, because it has one threshold to move. At the source the finite-sample correction is worth 0.47 of a coverage point against a between-draw standard deviation of 2.48 points.

Five encoders were compared for infarction: each was frozen, a linear probe fitted on its PTB-XL representations and the thresholds spent as in section 3.2, at an 80% target rather than the 90% used elsewhere. All five hold the target at home, between 79.8% and 80.0%. None reaches it at Chongqing, where they read between 14.9% and 75.1%, and four of the five over-cover at Shandong. Their discrimination spans an AUROC of 0.818 (95% CI 0.799 to 0.838) to 0.919 (0.907 to 0.932), both ends of that range being arms with no corpus of this study in their pre-training: the randomly initialised control and the strongest pretrained encoder. Every Chongqing figure is a held-out reading, no arm having been pretrained there. The middle of the range cannot be read, since three of the five arms had a corpus used here in their pre-training, one PTB-XL, one PTB-XL and Shandong together, and one Chapman-Shaoxing with Ningbo, so their PTB-XL AUROCs are not held-out figures and the five cannot be put in an order.

## Appendix H. How the cut-offs are computed, the reweighted option and the intervals

Both conformal schemes score a case by one minus the probability the model gives its label, the least-ambiguous-set score, and admit a label when that score falls at or below the ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉/n empirical quantile of the calibration scores, with α = 0.10 for the 90% target; the (n+1) is what makes the guarantee hold in finite samples rather than asymptotically. The single threshold and per-label calibration meet at the same miss rate on the ill by construction and not by measurement: the per-label threshold of the ill admits the disease above the point below which 10% of the ill calibration cases' scores fall, which is the single threshold's 90%-sensitivity point, the two taking the same finite-sample rank of the same scores.

Since two label probabilities sum to one, a set comes back empty only when the two thresholds admitting each label sum to less than one. At α = 0.10 on the infarction model they do not: the pooled scheme puts a single threshold at 0.566, and the per-label pair sits at 0.388 and 0.787, summing to 1.18. Every referral in the pooled and per-label schemes is therefore a both-label set; the weighted scheme is the exception. At an 80% target the two thresholds sum to less than one, so every deferral is an empty set rather than a both-label set, 13.4% of tracings at the source. Under the adaptive-prediction-sets score, also in the results files, the source-site infarction coverage reads 82.7% against 73.5%, bought with a much larger deferral rate.

The fourth scheme, label-shift weighting, reweights the calibration cases toward the target's class mix estimated by black-box shift estimation [26], with an asymptotic guarantee that depends on that estimate [3,4]; it was run on infarction and on the rotation. Its estimate was poor: 40.9% infarction at Chongqing against 14.9% observed, and 2.4% at Shandong against 1.0%. Reweighting also spends calibration data, the effective sample size falling from 1,099 to 967 at Chongqing and 864 at Shandong. Paired on the same draws it gained 0.1 points of infarction coverage at the source where per-label calibration gained 16.7, and at Shandong it lost 2.3 points and produced empty sets on 4.3% of tracings. An asymptotic guarantee resting on an estimated prior bought nothing here that an exact construction resting on none did not buy more cheaply.

For infarction, the spread across the 200 draws describes the calibration draw, not the sampling error of the cohorts, which is the larger quantity at the target sites. Where a claim rests on a difference, the interval comes from 2,000 replicates that resample the source patients before halving them and resample each target cohort as well, by record, since the score files carry no patient identifier; the scored cohorts are 25,770 tracings from 24,666 patients at Shandong and 17,955 from 17,018 at Chongqing. Named tests fail if a record-level split replaces the patient-level one, if any target label reaches a threshold, or if a published figure differs from its recomputation by an implementation that does not import the one under test. The quantities the discussion argues from (the plain reject rule, the finite-sample correction, the cases a site needs to measure its own coverage, the predictive value and AUROC at each site) are computed by `scripts/auxiliary.py` into `results/auxiliary.json`.

## Appendix I. The recording faults

`scripts/perturbations.py` writes `results/perturbations.json`; its unperturbed condition is scored through the same path and agrees with `results/baseline/scores.npz` to 3.0 × 10⁻⁷, so the differences are the fault's and not the pipeline's.

Scaling every lead by 0.8 left the AUROC unchanged to four decimals while 2,177 of the 2,198 records changed rank, Kendall's tau between the two score vectors being 0.930. The reordering is local and cancels over pairs: 1.6% of infarction and non-infarction pairs flip, in both directions, and the AUROC moves by 3 in the fifth decimal. The whole distribution moved, the median score rising from 0.104 to 0.140, so the threshold left where it was cut it somewhere else.

Baseline wander is the one fault the deferral rate shows, because it moves the bulk of the distribution onto the deferral band rather than past it: it compresses the scores, the standard deviation falling from 0.31 to 0.20, and shifts them, the median rising from 0.10 to 0.34, so that 30.8% of tracings land between the two per-label boundaries against 10.7% as recorded.
